Strategic Domain Decomposition

The EHR platform is decomposed into 6 bounded contexts, each representing a distinct business capability with its own data ownership, API surface, and event contracts. This page explains what each domain does, why it exists, and how they collaborate.

What is Domain Decomposition?

In a hospital, different departments handle different things — the front desk manages patient registration, the pharmacy fills prescriptions, the lab runs tests, and the scheduling desk books appointments. Each department has its own staff, its own records, and its own way of working. They collaborate by sending messages, not by sharing a single filing cabinet.

Domain Decomposition applies the same idea to software. Instead of one giant application that does everything (a "monolith"), we split the system into smaller, focused services — each one responsible for a single area of the hospital's workflow. In software architecture, we call each of these areas a Bounded Context.

  • Each context owns its data — Patient Management doesn't peek into Pharmacy's database, and vice versa
  • Each context has its own API — Other services or the frontend call it through well-defined endpoints
  • Contexts communicate via events — When a patient is registered, an event is published; scheduling and clinical pick it up
  • Teams can work independently — The lab team can deploy their service without waiting for the scheduling team
  • Failures are isolated — If the pharmacy service goes down, you can still check patient records and schedule appointments

Why Exactly 6 Bounded Contexts?

We analyzed the hospital workflow end-to-end — from the moment a patient walks in to the moment they leave with lab results and prescriptions in hand. The natural boundaries emerged around who uses the data, when they use it, and how often it changes.

The Patient Journey

A patient arrives → Patient Management captures their identity. The front desk books a slot → Scheduling. The doctor examines them → Clinical Encounters. The doctor prescribes medication → Pharmacy & Rx. Blood work is ordered → Laboratory. The doctor who treated them is tracked in → Provider Management.

The Technical Rationale

Each context has different scaling needs. Lab results come in bursts (morning blood draws); scheduling peaks at 8 AM when the switchboard opens; clinical records grow steadily. By separating them, we can scale each independently — more instances for scheduling during peak hours, more database capacity for the lab during result processing.

Patient Management

Core identity, demographics, insurance, clinical indicators

This is the source of truth for who the patient is. Every other service references Patient Management when it needs a patient's name, date of birth, insurance, or allergy list. Think of it as the hospital's master patient index.

Clinical Encounters

Medical encounters, diagnoses, treatment plans, clinical notes

When a patient sees a doctor, that visit is an Encounter. This context captures everything that happens during the visit — the doctor's notes, diagnoses (using ICD-10 codes), treatment plans, and medical records. It's the clinical heart of the EHR.

Scheduling handles the when and where of healthcare delivery. It manages appointment slots, checks provider availability, maintains waitlists for cancellations, and sends automated reminders so patients don't miss their visits.

Provider Management

Clinician profiles, credentials, departments

Provider Management is the source of truth for who delivers care. It tracks doctors, nurses, and specialists — their credentials, department assignments, and schedules. Other services reference this when they need to know who prescribed a medication or who is available for an appointment.

How Do These Contexts Talk to Each Other?

In a real hospital, departments don't share a single database or filing cabinet. They send messages: the lab sends results to the doctor, the doctor sends prescriptions to the pharmacy. Our software works the same way — through domain events published to a message bus (Apache Kafka / Azure Event Hubs).

Example: A doctor finishes an appointment and orders blood work. Here's what happens behind the scenes:

1. Clinical Encounters publishes an EncounterCompleted event
2. Laboratory receives the event and creates a new lab order
3. The lab tech processes the sample and records results
4. Laboratory publishes LabResultReady (or CriticalLabResult if abnormal)
5. Clinical Encounters picks up the result and attaches it to the patient's record
6. Notification Service sends an alert to the ordering physician

No service makes a direct database call to another. They communicate through events. This means if the lab system goes down temporarily, patients can still be registered, appointments can still be booked, and prescriptions can still be filled. The lab will catch up when it comes back online.

What is an Aggregate Root?

You'll see "Aggregate Root" labels on the entity tags above. Here's what that means in plain English:

An Aggregate is a cluster of related objects that are always changed together. The Aggregate Root is the "main" object — the entry point. You never modify a child object directly; you always go through the root.

Real-World Analogy

Think of a Patient as the Aggregate Root. A patient has allergies, vitals, and emergency contacts. You don't update an allergy in isolation — you update "Patient #123's allergy list." The Patient is the root; allergies and vitals are children that only make sense in the context of that patient.

Why It Matters

Aggregate Roots enforce consistency boundaries. When you save a patient, all their allergies and vitals are saved in one transaction. This prevents situations where a patient exists but their allergy list is only half-saved. It also tells the system where to draw the line when splitting into microservices.
